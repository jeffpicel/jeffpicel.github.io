Independent AI researcher · AI safety, reasoning and evaluation

I'm an independent AI researcher. Most of what I work on comes back to one question: how can we trust what an AI agent does when nobody can check every step? That splits into smaller questions. Can we tell, partway through, that an attempt is going wrong? What does training actually teach an agent, and what does it miss? And how do we measure any of this well enough to believe the answer? My first result: on tasks with risky actions, steering an agent with a learned critic still helps even after reinforcement learning. Read the research note.

What I'm working on

Only the first of these is written up so far. The others are placeholders for now.

  • Critics that guard and steer agents. Small networks that read an agent's internal state partway through a task and forecast how the attempt will end, used to steer it or stop it. I compare them with training the agent itself by reinforcement learning. In the first sealed test, on 1,700 planning puzzles, a critic on the untouched model matched reinforcement learning, and adding a critic on top of reinforcement learning raised success by about 6 points. A paper is in preparation. Around it sit three more questions: what picture of the task a reasoning model builds over a long attempt, and how that picture shapes what a critic can learn; when a critic should step in; and when an agent should stop spending compute. Read more
  • Learning from reward that generalizes. Reinforcement learning has worked very well within domains that have a checkable reward, such as math and code. Some of those gains carry over to nearby tasks, but they rarely travel far, so the field keeps building new training environments task by task. How can the gains from reward data carry across domains? I'm interested in signs that an agent has understood what matters, not just what was paid for: strategies, cognitive or physical, that carry over to new domains, that are learned quickly, or other markers of understanding. Part of this is whether a critic trained on some tasks still works on new ones. Notes coming.
  • Paying for what you can measure. Economics has long studied what happens when people are paid only for the part of their work that can be measured. What does that predict when we train AI on rewards that miss part of what we care about, such as safety? Two questions I want to test: does learning to cheat on one task spread to others, and can a separate monitor catch kinds of cheating it was never shown? Notes coming.

Ideas from the neuroscience of learning from reward run through all three.

Background

Before this I spent about seven years as a data scientist (DS) and machine learning (ML) engineer, first at Airbnb and then at Faire. At Faire I was the founding data team member (DS and ML/AI) for the brand side of the marketplace (Senior, promoted to Staff in 2023). I led the data science behind the system our Sales team used to choose which brands to pursue, and I conceived and led an ongoing measurement of the causal value brands added to the marketplace. For some time I was the data team tech lead for marketplace quality, which comes down to a trade-off: the cost of missing a harmful brand against the cost of penalizing a good one. To act on those calls, I used the production downranking systems our engineering team built. I also co-authored the company's first experimentation guide.

Before industry I did a PhD in economics at Harvard. My dissertation's lead paper used quirks in financial reporting, such as a past return rolling out of the 52-week window the Wall Street Journal printed, to find evidence that investors extrapolate from whichever past returns are most visible to them. Before that I studied mathematics and economics at the University of Chicago.
